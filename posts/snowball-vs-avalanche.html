How to choose snowball vs avalanche with your numbers

Snowball vs avalanche is a choice about order, not about paying a larger monthly total. You keep every minimum current, send extra money to one target, and then compare both orders with your own balances and rates.

Key takeaways

  • Both methods use the same monthly budget: all minimums plus the extra amount you can add.
  • Snowball sends that extra amount to the smallest balance first, so a debt may be gone sooner.
  • Avalanche sends the extra amount to the highest APR first, which usually lowers total interest.
  • The interest gap shrinks when your rates are close, and grows when one rate stands out.
  • Write both plans with your numbers, then pick the plan you will follow and update it each month.

Why snowball vs avalanche feels hard to pick

The two methods do the same basic job. You list every debt, pay each minimum, and put leftover money on a single target. When that target reaches zero, its old payment rolls to the next one.

The only design choice is the order. Snowball sorts by starting balance, smallest first. Avalanche sorts by APR, highest first. Ties can follow the order of your list.

Advice online often splits in two. One group wants the lowest interest cost. The other group wants an early closed account, because that win helps them stay with the plan. A thread in r/personalfinance titled “Snowball vs avalanche method.” (24 October 2025) is a clear example of that split: people want one right answer, and the replies point back to rates, balances, and what keeps a person paying.

There is no single order that fits every list. If your highest APR is also your smallest balance, the two orders start the same way. If a large, high-rate card sits next to a tiny, low-rate bill, the orders diverge.

This is planning math, not personal financial advice. A monthly estimate will not match a lender who charges interest daily, down to the cent.

How to compare both plans, step by step

  1. List each debt you want in the plan: a name, the current balance, the APR from your statement, and the minimum payment.
  2. Choose an extra amount you can add every month, on top of those minimums. If you are unsure, pick a number you can repeat, even a small one.
  3. Write two orders. For Snowball, sort by starting balance, smallest first. For Avalanche, sort by APR, highest first.
  4. For each order, walk month by month. Pay every minimum. Put the extra amount on the current target. Add estimated interest, apply the payment, and move to the next month.
  5. When a debt reaches zero, roll its minimum into the next target. Keep the monthly budget the same unless your real life changes it.
  6. At the end of each plan, write three results: the month the first debt is paid off, the month you are debt free, and the total interest.
  7. Pick one method. Log each payment. Once a month, copy the new statement balance into your list so the next month starts from real figures.

A workable interest estimate for this kind of plan is: starting balance times APR divided by 12, rounded to the cent, then subtract the payment. That is the model used in the sample later in this guide. Many lenders use daily interest, so treat the result as a plan, not a statement copy.

Common mistakes when you set the order

The first mistake is skipping a minimum on a debt that is not the current target. Both methods only work if every account stays current. The extra money is what changes the order, not a skipped bill.

The second mistake is letting a freed minimum disappear into spending. When a debt is paid off, that minimum should roll to the next target. If it does not, both plans slow down.

The third mistake is waiting for a perfect choice. If your APRs are close, the interest gap may be small. Start with one order, then switch later if you want to.

The fourth mistake is treating a spreadsheet as a lender. Update current balances from your statements. If a rate or minimum changes, change it in the plan too.

FAQ

Do I have to keep the same method for the whole plan?

No. You can switch after a debt is paid off, or sooner. Keep paying every minimum, and keep sending extra money to one target at a time.

What if two debts share an APR or a balance?

Pick a tie rule and use it on both lists. One simple rule is to follow the row order of your list. If two APRs match, you can also aim at the smaller balance first.

Will a plan match my lender statement?

It should be close, but not to the cent. A monthly estimate (balance times APR divided by 12) will differ from daily interest. Copy each new statement balance into your list once a month.

How many debts can I put in one plan?

The method works for a short list you can keep updated. The shop tracker linked below is built for up to 10 debts and a plan of up to 240 months.
