Write a yearly goal planner you can keep in 2027

A yearly goal planner works when it holds a few clear goals, a weekly action for each one, and a date to check progress. Start with a short look back, pick three goals you control, and break each goal into a first step, a weekly action, and four quarter milestones.

Key takeaways

  • Write a short list of goals, each with a finish line you can see.
  • Give every goal a first step this week and a weekly action you can repeat.
  • Add an if-then plan for the obstacle you already know is coming.
  • Check in every month, not only in December, and set one milestone per quarter.
  • Look back at the year you finished before you add new goals.

What a yearly goal planner should include

A useful planner is not a 365-page diary. It is a short system you can reopen in March and still understand. The pages that help most are a look back, a way to choose a few goals, one plan page per goal, a quarter view, and a monthly check-in.

People often lose steam after the first stretch of the year. A post in r/getdisciplined titled “How to set annual goals and actually stick to them” (written in late December 2024, as 2025 was about to start; page read 22 November 2026) describes that drop and argues for shorter checkpoints, such as a quarter, instead of one big deadline in December. You do not need that writer’s full framework. You do need a review date that comes around often enough to catch a stall.

Write each goal as something you control. “Save $3,000 for an emergency fund by 31 December 2027” can be planned. “Get a raise” depends on other people. “Be healthier” has no finish line until you say how you will know it is done.

How to fill the year, step by step

  1. Look back at 2026. Note what worked, what you want to stop, and what you never started.
  2. Rate the areas of your life that matter to you. The 2027 Goal Planner from Makerly Paper Co. uses eight life areas and a score from 1 to 10. Use those, or write your own list. Circle the areas you want to improve and can affect.
  3. Choose three goals. For each one, write why it matters in one or two sentences. If you cannot say why, drop it or rewrite it.
  4. Give each goal its own plan page: the goal in one sentence, how you will know it is done, a deadline, a first step, a weekly action, two if-then plans, and four quarter milestones.
  5. At the start of each quarter, set one milestone and one weekly action per goal. Tick a week only when you did the action.
  6. Once a month, score progress for each goal from 1 to 10 and write the next step. If a score stays flat for two months, change the weekly action or shrink the milestone.
  7. At the end of the year, write what you finished, what to carry forward, and what to leave behind.

Print at 100 percent, not “fit to page,” if you are working on paper. Plain printer paper is enough. If you prefer a tablet, use a PDF app that supports links and handwriting, so you can jump between the contents and each section.

Why yearly plans stall, and the usual mistakes

Plans stall when the list is too long. Ten goals look ambitious in January and unread by April. Three goals leave room for work, family, and rest, and they still fill a year.

Plans also stall when there is no weekly action. A deadline without a repeatable step is a wish. The weekly action should name what you will do and when, such as a transfer every Friday after payday.

A third stall is an obstacle you never wrote down. If an unplanned bill, a late night, or a lost week is likely, write the if-then plan while you are calm. Then you are not inventing a response in the middle of the week.

Common mistakes include goals you do not control, a vague finish line, skipping the look back, and buying a daily diary when you only needed a goal system. A dated calendar helps you check in. It does not replace the plan page.

A worked example from a sample goal plan

The sample below comes from the Example goal plan page in the 2027 Goal Planner by Makerly Paper Co., read from the product source on 22 November 2026 (Asia/Saigon). It is a filled-in sample, not a real person’s plan.

My goal: Save $3,000 for an emergency fund by 31 December 2027.

I will know it is done when: the savings account shows $3,000. Deadline: 31 December 2027.

Why this matters: so a car repair does not go on my credit card.

First step this week: open a separate savings account this Saturday.

Weekly action: move $58 every Friday after payday.

If-then plans: if an unplanned bill comes, then skip one week and add $10 to the next six. If I feel like giving up, then look at the progress bar on my monthly check-in.

Quarter milestones: Q1, open the savings account and save $750. Q2, reach $1,500. Q3, reach $2,250. Q4, reach $3,000.

Who can help: my sister. We text each other on the 1st. Reward: a weekend trip, paid from my normal budget.

Notice the shape, not only the topic. The goal names an amount and a date. The done test is something you can check. The weekly action has a day. The milestones add up to the same finish line. The if-then line tells you what to do when the plan wobbles.

FAQ

How many goals should I set?

The planner linked below is built for three goals. A short list is easier to review each month. If a fourth idea appears later, write it down and wait until a current goal is done or dropped.

Can I start after January?

Yes. In the dated planner linked below, the goal and quarter pages have no dates, so you can start in any month. The monthly pages are dated for 2027, with weeks starting on Sunday.

What paper size should I print?

Both US Letter and A4 are included as separate files. Print at actual size (100 percent) on plain paper.

Is this the same as a daily or weekly diary?

No. This is a goal system with look-back pages, plan pages, quarter plans, and monthly check-ins. It is not a 365-page daily diary.
